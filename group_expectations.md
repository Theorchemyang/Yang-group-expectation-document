# Yang Group Expectation Document

*Updated July 2026*

## Purpose of This Document

This document describes the general expectations, working principles, and culture of the Yang Group. Its purpose is to help current and prospective group members understand how we conduct research, communicate, collaborate, and support one another.

These guidelines are not an employment contract and do not replace policies of the Department of Chemistry, the Graduate School, UW–Madison, funding agencies, or applicable immigration authorities. When a conflict exists, the relevant official policy takes precedence.

This is a living document and may be updated as the group develops.

---

# 1. About the Group

The Yang Group is a theoretical and computational chemistry group with a strong emphasis on theoretical and methodological development.

Our research focuses on developing physically rigorous, computationally practical methods for studying quantum molecular systems, particularly nuclear quantum effects in chemistry and molecular simulations. A major focus is the development and application of the constrained nuclear–electronic orbital framework and related multicomponent quantum-chemistry methods.

Our primary goals are to:

* Develop new theoretical and computational methods.
* Translate theoretical ideas into robust and accessible computational tools.
* Apply these methods to scientifically important chemical problems.
* Understand the underlying physical mechanisms rather than treating computations as black boxes.
* Train students and postdoctoral researchers to become independent, careful, and creative scientists.

Although method development is central to the group, practical applications are equally important. Applications test the strengths and limitations of our methods, reveal new scientific questions, and demonstrate how the methods can be useful to the broader scientific community.

We value physical insight, mathematical rigor, computational reliability, intellectual openness, collaboration, research integrity, and mutual respect.

# 2. Character and Academic Integrity

Before learning how to become a scholar, one must first learn to be a decent person.

Scientific ability and research productivity are important, but they can never substitute for integrity, kindness, responsibility, and respect for others. Being an upright, kind, and trustworthy person is a basic requirement for membership in the group.

Every group member is expected to be:

* **Honest and principled:** Follow scientific principles even when doing so is inconvenient or leads to an unexpected or unfavorable result.
* **Upright and trustworthy:** Keep commitments, report work truthfully, admit mistakes, and never take unfair advantage of others.
* **Kind and respectful:** Treat colleagues, collaborators, staff members, and students with consideration, regardless of their position, background, or level of experience.
* **Responsible:** Take ownership of one’s work and consider how one’s actions affect collaborators and the group as a whole.
* **Fair:** Give proper credit to others and avoid claiming another person’s ideas, work, or contributions as one’s own.

Academic integrity is a fundamental and nonnegotiable requirement.

Group members must never:

* Fabricate or falsify results.
* Manipulate data or figures in a misleading way.
* Plagiarize text, ideas, code, or results.
* Misrepresent their own contributions or those of others.
* Conceal important negative findings, errors, or methodological limitations.
* Knowingly make scientific claims that are unsupported by evidence.
* Selectively report results merely because they support a preferred conclusion.

Scientific conclusions must follow from the evidence, not from what we hope or expect the results to show. Unexpected, negative, or inconclusive results should be reported honestly. Such results may still be scientifically meaningful and may reveal important limitations of a method or hypothesis.

Mistakes are a normal part of research. Members are expected to acknowledge mistakes, inform relevant collaborators, and correct them promptly. Making an honest mistake is fundamentally different from concealing a mistake or deliberately presenting misleading information.

Authorship, acknowledgment, and scientific credit must fairly reflect actual contributions. Members should recognize the intellectual, computational, technical, and mentoring contributions of others. Seniority does not justify taking credit from a junior colleague, and junior members should likewise acknowledge the guidance and assistance they receive.

No scientific accomplishment can compensate for dishonesty or serious mistreatment of others. Being a good scientist begins with being a person of integrity.

# 3. Before and Shortly After Joining the Group

## 3.1 Academic Preparation

Research in a theory group requires more than learning how to run computational software. Group members should develop a strong understanding of the physical principles, mathematical foundations, numerical methods, and approximations underlying their calculations.

Coursework is therefore an important part of research training, especially during the first two years.

The following courses are generally expected for graduate students:

| Course                                      | Recommended Year | General Expectation                                                |
| ------------------------------------------- | ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| CHEM 661: Statistical Mechanics             | Year 1           | Strong mastery of the material                                     |
| CHEM 675: Quantum Mechanics                 | Year 1           | Strong mastery of the material                                     |
| CHEM 775: Electronic Structure of Molecules | Year 1 or 2      | Strongly recommended, particularly for method-development projects |

Students should normally aim for an A-level understanding in these core theory courses. However, grades are not the only measure of understanding or research potential. Course selections may be adjusted based on a student’s previous training, research direction, teaching responsibilities, and discussions with Yang.

Depending on the project, additional preparation in mathematics, physics, computer science, numerical analysis, machine learning, or scientific programming may be recommended.

## 3.2 Computational Skills

New members should gradually become comfortable with:

* Linux and high-performance computing environments.
* Python and other programming languages relevant to their projects.
* Electronic-structure and molecular-simulation software.
* Version control, particularly Git.
* Data analysis and scientific visualization.
* Writing clear documentation and reproducible computational workflows.

It is normal to enter the group without mastering all these skills. What matters is a willingness to learn and to seek help when necessary.

## 3.3 Initial Research Period

During the first several months, new members will generally:

* Read foundational papers related to their projects.
* Learn the necessary theoretical and computational background.
* Reproduce selected previous calculations or results.
* Become familiar with the relevant software and group code.
* Develop an initial research plan with Yang and other project mentors.

The pace of this process will differ among individuals. Members should not compare themselves excessively with others, particularly during the early stages of training.

# 4. Working in the Group

## 4.1 Funding and Appointments

Graduate students may be supported through teaching assistantships, research assistantships, fellowships, or combinations of these sources. The group currently receives research support from programs including the NSF CAREER program, NSF CSSI Elements, and DOE SciDAC.

Appointment decisions depend on available funding, grant requirements, project alignment, departmental teaching needs, and the student’s training and professional-development needs. A TA or RA appointment is not a measure of a student’s importance or scientific ability.

Teaching is an important part of graduate education and provides valuable experience in communication, leadership, and fundamental chemistry. At the same time, Yang will make reasonable efforts to provide RA support when appropriate funding is available so that students have sufficient time to focus on research.

Students supported on research grants are expected to make appropriate progress on work related to the goals of those grants. All appointments and assigned duties must follow University, departmental, and funding-agency policies.

## 4.2 General Research Expectations

Research progress is not measured simply by the number of hours spent in the office. Focused and thoughtful work is more valuable than long but unproductive hours. Nevertheless, successful theoretical research requires sustained effort, regular communication, and intellectual engagement.

Group members are expected to:

* Take ownership of their research projects.
* Maintain steady and visible progress.
* Understand the theory and assumptions behind their calculations.
* Read the relevant literature continuously.
* Keep careful records of calculations, code changes, and scientific decisions.
* Analyze unexpected or negative results rather than hiding them.
* Ask for help before a problem causes an extended loss of time.
* Respond constructively to feedback.
* Gradually become more independent in identifying problems and proposing solutions.

Temporary periods of slow progress are normal. Research is difficult, and not every idea will work. Careful negative results can still be scientifically valuable. However, repeatedly avoiding difficult problems, failing to communicate, or reporting progress inaccurately is not acceptable.

## 4.3 Study, Research Hours, and Office Presence

The group does not use office hours as the primary measure of productivity. Members have different working styles, project needs, teaching schedules, and personal circumstances.

At the same time, in-person interaction is important for discussion, collaboration, mentoring, and maintaining a healthy group environment. Graduate students and postdoctoral researchers are therefore normally expected to be physically present for a substantial portion of the workweek.

As a general guideline, members should aim to be present in the office for an average of at least five hours per weekday. The preferred core collaboration windows are:

* 9:30–11:30 a.m.
* 2:30–5:30 p.m.

These are the times when Yang and other group members are most likely to initiate informal project discussions.

This is not a time-clock requirement. Classes, teaching duties, seminars, appointments, conferences, approved remote work, family responsibilities, illness, and other reasonable circumstances may affect an individual’s schedule. Members with a substantially different regular schedule should discuss it with Yang and keep their collaborators informed.

Working from home occasionally may be appropriate for tasks requiring uninterrupted concentration. However, a consistently remote arrangement should be discussed in advance and must comply with University requirements.

## 4.4 Learning and Differences in Background

Group members will enter with different levels of preparation in chemistry, physics, mathematics, and programming. These differences are normal and should not be a source of embarrassment.

A person who needs additional time to build the necessary background should invest that time without feeling ashamed. Serious and sustained effort deserves respect, even when progress is gradual. In contrast, avoiding foundational learning and relying only on quick results will eventually limit scientific development.

Members who already have stronger preparation should remain humble and help create an environment in which others can learn comfortably.

## 4.5 Meetings and Discussions with Yang

Discussion is essential to theoretical research. Group members are encouraged to speak with Yang frequently rather than waiting until a problem becomes serious.

Regular individual research meetings will be arranged based on the member’s project and stage of training. In addition, Yang will often visit the group offices for informal discussions.

When Yang’s office door is open and no meeting is in progress, group members are generally welcome to stop by. For discussions requiring substantial time, scheduling a meeting may be more effective.

Research updates should normally cover:

* What has been accomplished since the previous discussion.
* Important results and how they were interpreted.
* Problems or uncertainties that have arisen.
* Proposed solutions or alternative directions.
* Concrete next steps.

Brief written summaries, slides, figures, or notes are often helpful. Yang may not remember every detail from previous discussions, particularly when several projects are active. Maintaining shared notes and sending concise written follow-ups will help preserve continuity and reduce misunderstandings.

Members should approach scientific discussions with optimism, honesty, and an open mind. Disagreement about scientific questions is normal and often productive. Criticism should focus on the work and ideas, not the individual.

## 4.6 Group Meetings

Attendance at scheduled group meetings is expected unless a member has a class, teaching responsibility, approved absence, illness, or another unavoidable conflict.

Members should:

* Arrive on time and remain engaged.
* Prepare carefully when presenting.
* Explain both the scientific motivation and technical details of their work.
* Share incomplete work when feedback would be useful.
* Ask questions and provide constructive suggestions.
* Respect the presenter and allow junior members space to participate.

A group meeting is not an examination. Its purpose is to improve the science, share knowledge, identify problems, and help one another make progress.

## 4.7 Mentoring Within the Group

Postdoctoral researchers and senior graduate students may be asked to help mentor junior members. Mentoring may include explaining theory, reviewing code, troubleshooting calculations, helping interpret results, or advising on scientific communication.

Mentors should guide rather than simply provide answers or perform another person’s work. Mentees remain responsible for understanding their projects and should acknowledge the contributions of those who help them.

Mentoring is a group contribution and will be considered when evaluating professional development, authorship, and readiness for future leadership roles. However, the primary responsibility for supervision remains with Yang, and junior members should always feel free to approach him directly.

## 4.8 Collaboration

Collaborative research requires flexibility, reliability, and respect for people with different backgrounds and working styles.

Group members should:

* Agree on responsibilities, timelines, and expected outputs at the beginning of a collaboration.
* Respond to collaborators within a reasonable period.
* Inform collaborators early when a deadline may not be met.
* Avoid sharing unpublished results outside the collaboration without permission.
* Credit all intellectual and technical contributions appropriately.
* Make reasonable efforts to use tools and file formats accessible to collaborators.

LaTeX may be convenient for theoretical work, but not all collaborators use it. When collaborators prefer Microsoft Word or another format, group members should accommodate them rather than imposing LaTeX.

Authorship and author order should reflect actual contributions and should be discussed openly as projects develop. First authors are normally expected to take primary responsibility for the research, analysis, manuscript preparation, revisions, and communication among coauthors. Authorship decisions may change if contributions change substantially.

## 4.9 Code, Data, and Reproducibility

Research products created in the group should be organized so that another qualified group member can understand and reproduce the work.

Members are expected to:

* Keep code in an appropriate shared or backed-up repository.
* Use version control for substantial software development.
* Document important scripts, inputs, parameters, and computational procedures.
* Preserve the data and input files underlying publications.
* Record the software versions and computational environments used.
* Avoid keeping the only copy of important code or data on a personal computer.
* Follow licensing, confidentiality, and data-management requirements.

Before leaving the group, members must ensure that their code, data, documentation, and project history are transferred in an organized and usable form.

## 4.10 Research Integrity in Practice and Use of AI Tools

The principles in Section 2 apply to every stage of research, including calculations, coding, data analysis, figure preparation, manuscript writing, peer review, and communication with collaborators.

Members must preserve original data and maintain sufficient records to explain how reported results were obtained. Data or figures may be processed to improve clarity, but processing must not distort the scientific meaning of the results. Important methodological choices, uncertainties, and limitations should be disclosed.

Members should not omit calculations simply because they contradict a desired conclusion. When a result appears unusually favorable, it should be checked especially carefully.

Generative AI and related tools may be useful for language editing, coding assistance, brainstorming, or organizing information. However:

* The researcher remains responsible for the correctness of all content.
* AI-generated scientific statements, references, equations, and code must be independently checked.
* Confidential manuscripts, proposals, unpublished collaborator data, or restricted information should not be uploaded to unauthorized systems.
* AI tools should not replace the researcher’s own understanding or analysis.
* AI-generated text should not be represented as evidence of scientific understanding.
* The use of AI tools should be disclosed when required by a journal, funding agency, course, collaborator, or University policy.

When uncertain, members should discuss the proposed use of an AI tool with Yang.

# 5. Time Off, Holidays, and Personal Well-Being

## 5.1 General Principles

Rest and time away from research are important for sustained creativity and productivity. Group members are encouraged to take reasonable time off and should not feel guilty for doing so.

Formal leave entitlements differ according to appointment type and are governed by University policy. Group practices cannot reduce or replace any leave provided by the University.

Planned absences should be discussed with Yang in advance. Members should also coordinate with collaborators and address time-sensitive teaching, research, travel, or manuscript responsibilities before leaving.

## 5.2 Group Holiday Practices

As a general group practice:

* Thanksgiving break normally includes Thursday through Sunday.
* The group generally operates at a reduced pace during the year-end period, approximately December 22 through January 4.
* University-observed legal holidays will be respected according to the rules applicable to each appointment.
* Spring recess is not automatically a group vacation, although members may request time off during this period.

These group practices do not create additional paid leave beyond what is available under the member’s appointment. Members should record formal leave when required.

## 5.3 Vacation and Extended Travel

Members should discuss ordinary vacation plans as early as reasonably possible. Longer absences require additional planning, especially when experiments, software releases, paper deadlines, teaching duties, or collaborative projects are active.

Extended home visits may be possible for international students and postdoctoral researchers. Such visits should normally be discussed several months in advance and must be consistent with University leave policies, appointment obligations, visa requirements, research progress, and available funding.

Rather than informally “banking” group vacation days, members should follow the official leave and time-off rules associated with their appointments.

## 5.4 Illness, Family Needs, and Emergencies

Health and family emergencies should be treated seriously. Members should notify Yang as soon as reasonably possible but do not need to disclose private medical details beyond what is necessary for arranging work or leave.

Members should consult the appropriate departmental administrator, Human Resources office, Graduate School, or International Student Services when formal leave, accommodations, or immigration questions arise.

# 6. Conferences and Professional Travel

Presenting research at conferences is an important part of scientific and professional development. The group will make reasonable efforts to support conference participation when the member has appropriate work to present and funding is available.

As a general goal, graduate students and postdoctoral researchers making satisfactory progress should have regular opportunities to attend conferences. The exact frequency will depend on research progress, the relevance of the meeting, available funding, grant restrictions, and the needs of other group members.

Conference plans should normally be made at least six months in advance. Members are expected to:

* Discuss the proposed conference with Yang before submitting an abstract or making reservations.
* Submit abstracts and register before early deadlines when possible.
* Apply for departmental, Graduate School, conference, and professional-society travel awards.
* Use reasonable and economical travel arrangements.
* Follow all University travel and reimbursement requirements.
* Prepare presentations carefully and practice them in advance.
* Represent the group and UW–Madison professionally.

Conference support is not determined solely by whether a student is currently appointed as a TA or RA. Funding decisions will consider the scientific purpose of the trip, available resources, and equitable opportunities across the group.

# 7. Publications and Scientific Communication

Publications are an important outcome of research, but paper counts alone do not define scientific success.

Group members should aim to produce work that is:

* Scientifically meaningful.
* Technically reliable.
* Clearly communicated.
* Reproducible.
* Honest about approximations and limitations.
* Useful to the broader scientific community.

The primary student or postdoctoral researcher on a project is normally expected to lead the first manuscript draft. Drafts do not need to be perfect before being shared, but they should represent a serious effort and contain the necessary scientific content.

All coauthors should have a reasonable opportunity to review a manuscript before submission. Comments should be addressed carefully, and major disagreements should be discussed openly.

Members should not delay writing until every possible calculation has been completed. Writing often reveals gaps in reasoning and should be considered part of the research process.

# 8. Graduate-Student Progress and Graduation

## 8.1 General Progress

Progress should be evaluated through the total development of the student, including:

* Understanding of the scientific field.
* Mastery of theoretical and computational methods.
* Quality and reliability of research.
* Ability to work independently.
* Creativity in identifying and solving problems.
* Scientific writing and presentation.
* Collaboration and mentoring.
* Completion of program requirements.

Students and Yang should discuss long-term goals and professional development regularly. As students advance, they should take increasing responsibility for defining research questions, planning projects, and making scientific decisions.

## 8.2 General Graduation Expectations

A graduate student will normally be ready to graduate after:

* Completing the formal requirements of the Chemistry PhD program, including the Thesis Background Examination and Research Proposal examination.
* Developing a coherent and substantial body of original research.
* Demonstrating the ability to conduct research with an appropriate level of independence.
* Completing or bringing to an appropriate stopping point the major projects associated with the dissertation.
* Preparing a satisfactory dissertation and successfully defending it.
* Organizing and transferring relevant code, data, documentation, and unfinished project materials.
* Helping train another group member to continue an ongoing project when appropriate.

As a general benchmark, students should aim to have approximately three substantial first-author or co-first-author manuscripts published, accepted, submitted, or close to submission by the time of graduation. This is a guideline rather than an automatic or inflexible rule.

The appropriate publication record will depend on the scientific scope and difficulty of the projects, the student’s contributions, the quality and completeness of the work, collaboration timelines, and circumstances outside the student’s control. Three papers do not automatically guarantee graduation, and fewer than three papers do not automatically prevent it.

Graduation timing should be discussed well in advance, ideally beginning at least six to twelve months before the anticipated defense.

# 9. Postdoctoral Researchers

Postdoctoral researchers are expected to operate with greater independence than graduate students while continuing to develop scientifically and professionally.

In addition to conducting high-quality research, postdoctoral researchers should normally:

* Help shape the direction of their projects.
* Contribute ideas for new methods, applications, or proposals.
* Mentor students when appropriate.
* Participate actively in group discussions.
* Lead manuscript preparation for their projects.
* Develop software and research products that remain usable after their departure.
* Work with Yang on an appropriate career-development plan.

The group will support reasonable professional-development activities, including conference participation, job applications, proposal writing, networking, and preparation for academic or nonacademic careers.

# 10. Professional Conduct and Group Culture

Every member has a responsibility to help create a respectful and supportive research environment.

Group members should:

* Treat others professionally regardless of seniority, background, identity, or research experience.
* Give credit generously and accurately.
* Avoid gossip, personal attacks, intimidation, or dismissive behavior.
* Respect reasonable personal boundaries and working differences.
* Provide criticism constructively.
* Admit mistakes and correct them promptly.
* Help colleagues without creating unhealthy dependence.
* Celebrate one another’s accomplishments without turning research into constant competition.

Scientific standards should be high, but group members should not be afraid to ask basic questions, show incomplete work, or acknowledge uncertainty.

Concerns about group interactions, mentoring, workload, research conduct, or personal treatment may be raised directly with Yang. Members may also consult departmental leadership, graduate-program staff, Human Resources, the Graduate School, or other appropriate University resources.

# 11. Leaving or Switching Groups

## 11.1 Switching Research Groups

A student who concludes that the group is not a good fit, or who develops substantially different research interests, should speak with Yang directly.

Different research groups have different scientific focuses, mentoring styles, and cultures. A student may not thrive in one environment but may succeed very well in another. Switching groups should not be treated as a personal failure.

Yang will make reasonable efforts to help the student explore alternatives and transition professionally. Raising the possibility of switching groups will not result in retaliation.

## 11.2 Departing the Group

Before graduating or otherwise leaving, members should:

* Complete an organized transfer of research data, code, and documentation.
* Explain the current status and next steps of unfinished projects.
* Return group and University equipment.
* Resolve manuscript responsibilities and expected authorship.
* Provide reliable future contact information.
* Follow confidentiality, intellectual-property, and data-retention requirements.

Members who have left the group remain entitled to appropriate recognition for their contributions. They are also expected to remain reasonably responsive when their assistance is needed to complete papers arising from their work.

The group hopes to maintain positive long-term relationships with its alumni and to continue supporting their professional development after they leave.
